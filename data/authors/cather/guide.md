# Working Guide to This Author's Prose

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences vary widely, from short declarative statements of five to eight words to complex constructions running forty words or more. The longer sentences accumulate through serial observation, piling concrete details in sequence rather than through subordination. A typical pattern: a short, blunt assertion followed by a longer sentence that expands with sensory specifics. The rhythm often slows at paragraph ends, settling into a reflective or summarizing cadence.

## Syntax Habits
The author favors compound sentences joined by "and" or "but," creating a cumulative, paratactic effect. Appositives are frequent, often set off by commas or dashes, adding character or scene details in compressed form. Parallel structures appear in lists of three or four items, especially when describing physical traits or actions. The author sometimes inverts subject-verb order for emphasis, particularly in landscape descriptions.

## Diction and Register
Vocabulary is concrete and sensory, drawn from domestic life, farming, and small-town commerce. Abstract nouns are rare; when they appear, they are immediately grounded in physical particulars. The register is plain but not simple—words like "dissolute," "licentiousness," and "sanctimoniousness" sit alongside "hired girls" and "cornfields." Regional and ethnic markers appear in dialogue but not in narration.

## Punctuation
Dashes are used liberally for interruption, afterthought, and emphasis. Semicolons join closely related independent clauses, especially in character sketches. Commas separate items in series but also mark off participial phrases and appositives. Quotation marks are used sparingly, often for a single striking phrase rather than extended speech.

## Figurative Habits
Similes are frequent and drawn from the natural world or domestic life: faces compared to rocks worn by water, the moon likened to a galleon, teeth described as factory-made. Metaphors are rare and brief. Personification appears occasionally for natural elements—the river "undermined" and "bit out" land. Figures tend to be homely rather than elevated, comparing the unfamiliar to the familiar.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs are of medium length, typically five to twelve sentences. Each paragraph focuses on a single character, scene, or idea, often opening with a topic sentence that names the subject. Transitions between paragraphs are abrupt, moving from one character or location to another without connective tissue. Within paragraphs, the author moves from general observation to specific detail.

## Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is rendered in plain, idiomatic speech with regional and ethnic markers. Speakers are identified by tags like "said" or "asked," rarely by elaborate attributions. Extended exchanges are broken by brief narrative interruptions—a gesture, a glance, a shift in posture. Some dialogue is reported indirectly, especially when summarizing a character's habitual speech.

## Narration and Perspective
First-person and third-person limited are both used. The narrator is often a retrospective observer, looking back on youth from adulthood. In third-person passages, the perspective stays close to a single character, filtering events through that character's perceptions and judgments. The author shifts between external observation and internal reflection, but never enters a character's mind for extended interior monologue.

## Does Not
1. Does not use stream of consciousness or free indirect discourse for extended passages.
2. Does not employ flashbacks or non-linear chronology.
3. Does not write dialogue without attribution tags.
4. Does not use figurative language drawn from technology or urban life.
5. Does not include authorial commentary or moralizing in the narrator's voice.
6. Does not describe sexual encounters explicitly.
7. Does not use dialect spelling to mark non-standard speech.
8. Does not end chapters or sections with cliffhangers or dramatic revelations.
