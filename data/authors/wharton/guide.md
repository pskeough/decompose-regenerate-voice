# Working Guide: Analysis of Prose Habits

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences oscillate between clipped fragments of dialogue (often a single word or short phrase) and elaborate periodic constructions running forty to sixty words. The longer sentences accumulate qualifying clauses before reaching their main verb, creating a delayed resolution. Scene-setting passages employ longer, more measured rhythms, while dialogue and moments of emotional intensity break into shorter, more jagged units.

## Syntax Habits
Subordinate clauses introduced by "though," "as," "if," and "while" frequently open sentences or are inserted parenthetically. The author uses paired constructions extensively: "not only...but," "not so much...as," "not...but," creating parallel structures that define through negation. Appositives and participial phrases provide compressed backstory. Interrogative constructions are used to advance narrative tension without direct statement.

## Diction and Register
Vocabulary operates in two registers: a formal, Latinate diction for narration ("tergiversations," "valetudinarian," "indissolubility") and a colloquial, idiomatic register for dialogue ("Gad," "by Jove," "flat failure"). The author freely merges these, placing formal language in characters' casual speech and colloquialisms in narrative summary. Abstract nouns predominate in psychological passages.

## Punctuation
Parentheses and dashes proliferate, often nested within each other. Em-dashes mark hesitation, interruption, and trailing-off in dialogue. Semicolons link closely related independent clauses, especially in passages of interior analysis. Commas separate serial phrases and mark off parenthetical interruptions. Exclamation points appear in dialogue but rarely in narrative.

## Figurative Habits
Similes and metaphors draw from three domains: commerce and finance ("currency," "investment"), domestic objects and processes ("dusting," "cracked side to the wall," "china-mender"), and natural cycles ("falling of leaves," "spring sky"). Figurative language tends to be ironic or deflating rather than elevating. Extended metaphors occasionally run through a paragraph but are typically compressed into single phrases.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs in narrative sections range from five to fifteen sentences, with topic sentences often stating a general observation before moving to specific illustration. Dialogue paragraphs are short, frequently a single speech or stage direction. Emotional climaxes receive their own paragraph. Transitions between paragraphs often employ contrast markers ("but," "yet," "meanwhile").

## Dialogue Handling
Stage directions in parentheses interrupt speech, indicating tone and gesture. Characters often repeat or echo each other's words. Interruptions and pauses are marked explicitly. Characters rarely complete sentences; trailing dashes indicate unfinished thoughts. Speech is used to reveal subtext—what is avoided matters as much as what is said.

## Narration and Perspective
Third-person limited perspective, closely tied to a single consciousness per scene. The narrator can enter the character's mind for indirect thought ("he thought," "she felt") but maintains ironic distance through qualifying phrases ("as if," "seemed," "appeared"). Free indirect discourse merges character's voice with narrator's. Shifts between characters occur only at scene breaks.

## Does Not
1. Use omniscient narration that comments independently
2. Write passages of pure action without psychological commentary
3. Employ dialect or phonetic spelling for characters
4. Describe physical appearance of characters in extended detail
5. Use present tense except in dialogue stage directions
6. End scenes or chapters with explicit summary
7. Introduce characters through direct exposition before action begins
8. Use metaphors drawn from technology or industrial processes
