# Working Guide to This Author's Prose Style

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences vary widely, from clipped dialogue fragments of three or four words to descriptive sentences running thirty to fifty words. Long sentences accumulate through coordination with "and" and "but," and through participial phrases that add successive actions or perceptions. Short sentences often deliver emotional climaxes or physical actions, creating a staccato punch after longer accumulations.

## Syntax Habits
The author frequently uses present participles to open sentences or clauses, creating a sense of ongoing action ("Still clutching the boy, he dragged him"). Inverted constructions appear for emphasis ("Into her eyes came the absent look"). Dialogue is rendered phonetically, with dropped consonants and altered vowels to indicate dialect, but narrative prose remains standard. The author often places a short, blunt statement after a longer, more elaborate one for contrast.

## Diction and Register
Narrative diction is precise but plain, favoring concrete nouns and active verbs over abstraction. Regional and class markers appear in dialogue through phonetic spelling and grammatical nonstandard forms ("w'at," "donc," "yonda"). The narrator's voice is educated but not elevated, using words like "piquant," "frouzy," and "lucid" without ostentation. Physical sensations and objects dominate the vocabulary.

## Punctuation
Dashes are frequent, used to mark abrupt shifts, interruptions, or afterthoughts. Commas separate items in series and set off participial phrases. Semicolons appear occasionally to link closely related independent clauses. Exclamation points are used sparingly, mostly in dialogue. Quotation marks enclose speech, with attribution often placed after the first clause.

## Figurative Habits
Similes are common and often draw from the natural world or domestic life ("like a dog in pain," "like some dim, gaunt monster"). Metaphors are rare and brief when they appear. The author compares emotional states to physical sensations (a heart beating "like a muffled hammer"). Personification is occasional and understated. Figurative language tends to be functional rather than decorative.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs are short to medium length, rarely exceeding eight sentences. Dialogue creates frequent paragraph breaks, with each speaker's turn getting its own paragraph. Narrative paragraphs often focus on a single character's perception or action. Transitions between paragraphs are often abrupt, moving to a new time, place, or character without explicit bridging.

## Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is rendered with heavy phonetic spelling for regional speech, including dropped consonants ("an'" for "and," "w'en" for "when") and altered vowels ("yonda" for "yonder"). Attribution is simple ("he said," "she returned"). Dialogue often carries the burden of character revelation and plot advancement. Characters interrupt themselves and each other. Interior monologue is rare; thoughts are usually reported indirectly.

## Narration and Perspective
Third-person limited, closely tied to a single character's perceptions and thoughts per scene. The narrator reports what characters see, hear, and feel, but does not comment omnisciently. Shifts in focal character occur between scenes or chapters, not within them. The narrator occasionally offers a summary judgment ("He did not know it himself; but Chicot knew so little") but stays close to the character's experience.

## Does Not
- Use elaborate or periodic sentence structures
- Employ extensive interior monologue or stream of consciousness
- Write lengthy descriptive passages of setting without human action
- Use formal or Latinate diction in narrative voice
- Include authorial asides or direct addresses to the reader
- Shift perspective within a single scene
- Use metaphor as a primary figurative device
- Write dialogue without dialect markers for regional speakers
