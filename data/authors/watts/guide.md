# Working Guide to This Author's Prose

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences range from single words ("I woke.") to sprawling constructions exceeding sixty words, often accumulating clauses through commas and conjunctions. Short declarative sentences punctuate longer passages, creating staccato emphasis. The rhythm shifts between rapid-fire dialogue exchanges and dense, meditative paragraphs that slow the reader through accumulation.

## Syntax Habits
The author favors subordinate clauses opening with *that*, *which*, and *where*, often stacking three or more in succession. Appositives appear frequently, set off by commas or dashes, to rename or clarify. Interrogative sentences are common, sometimes left unanswered. Parallel structures recur, especially in lists and rhetorical questions ("Think of... Think of...").

## Diction and Register
Technical and scientific vocabulary sits alongside colloquial speech and profanity. Abstract nouns (*consciousness*, *sentience*, *information*) are treated as concrete. The register shifts between clinical observation and visceral, almost poetic description. Characters speak in distinct idiolects—one uses precise, academic language; another favors blunt, emotional outbursts.

## Punctuation
Em-dashes are heavily used for interruption, afterthought, and dramatic pause—sometimes multiple times within a single sentence. Ellipses indicate trailing thoughts or hesitation. Colons introduce explanations or lists. Semicolons link closely related independent clauses. Italics emphasize words or indicate internal emphasis. Quotation marks frame direct speech, but internal monologue often runs without them.

## Figurative Habits
Similes and metaphors are frequent, often drawn from biology, mechanics, or the grotesque: creatures compared to "creatures in a draining tidal pool," ships compared to "a harpooned whale." Personification of technology and abstract concepts is common. Extended metaphors sometimes run across paragraphs. The figurative language tends toward the visceral and unsettling.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs vary dramatically in length—from single sentences to dense blocks of several hundred words. Topic sentences are rare; paragraphs often begin mid-thought or with a character's action. Dialogue is typically given its own paragraph, with attribution woven into the action. Longer paragraphs accumulate clauses and observations before breaking at a shift in focus or speaker.

## Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is rendered with minimal attribution tags, often embedded in action or description. Characters interrupt each other frequently, marked by em-dashes. Internal thoughts and external speech sometimes blur without clear demarcation. Dialogue serves both exposition and character revelation, with subtext often more important than surface meaning.

## Narration and Perspective
First-person narration, intimate and confessional, with frequent self-interruption and self-doubt. The narrator is an observer who reflects on his own limitations. The perspective shifts between immediate sensory experience and abstract philosophical rumination. Time moves fluidly, with flashbacks and internal digressions.

## Does Not
- Use omniscient narration
- Provide clear chapter breaks or section markers
- Employ conventional romantic or sentimental language
- Use footnotes or endnotes
- Describe characters' physical appearances in detail
- Include humor or comic relief
- Use straightforward chronological narrative without interruption
- Resolve ambiguity or provide clear answers
