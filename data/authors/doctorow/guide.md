# Working Guide to This Author's Prose

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences alternate between clipped, punchy constructions of five to ten words and longer, sprawling sentences that run thirty to fifty words. The short sentences deliver decisive actions or blunt observations. The long sentences accumulate details through chains of clauses, often stacking three or four observations before reaching a period. Dialogue tags are typically short and inserted mid-sentence or after brief exchanges.

## Syntax Habits
The author frequently uses present participles to open sentences or clauses, creating a sense of ongoing action. Appositives and parenthetical asides appear regularly, set off by commas or dashes, to layer in character observations or contextual details. Parallel structure appears in lists of three or four items, often without a final conjunction. The author also uses "and" to connect independent clauses in a cumulative, breathless rhythm rather than subordinating one action to another.

## Diction and Register
Diction shifts sharply between registers within a single passage. Technical jargon (networking terms, game mechanics, legal phrases) sits alongside colloquial speech, profanity, and slang. Characters use contractions heavily. The narrator adopts a neutral, slightly detached tone that occasionally dips into dry humor or understatement. Brand names and specific product references appear without explanation.

## Punctuation
Dashes are the dominant punctuation mark—used for interruptions, afterthoughts, and shifts in direction. Commas are frequent but not always grammatically necessary; they create a conversational, additive rhythm. Ellipses are rare. Quotation marks enclose dialogue, but internal thoughts appear in italics without tags. Colons introduce lists or explanations.

## Figurative Habits
Similes are concrete and often draw from mundane or unpleasant physical experiences: body odor, hunger, exhaustion. Metaphors are extended but grounded in familiar objects (Russian dolls, doors slamming, wind tunnels). The author avoids ornate or literary comparisons; figurative language serves to clarify a character's physical or emotional state rather than to decorate.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs are short, often two to five sentences. Dialogue exchanges break into separate paragraphs for each speaker, with minimal attribution. Action paragraphs are compressed: a character moves, observes, reacts, and the paragraph ends. Longer expository passages are broken by single-sentence paragraphs that function as beats or shifts in focus.

## Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is terse and often overlaps with action. Characters interrupt each other. Tags are simple ("he said," "she typed") and placed after the first few words or mid-sentence. Subtext is conveyed through what characters do while speaking—looking away, shrugging, compressing lips—rather than through explicit emotional description. Typed or texted dialogue appears in monospaced formatting with angle brackets.

## Narration and Perspective
Third-person limited, shifting between characters within a scene but staying with one per passage. The narrator has access to the focal character's physical sensations (hunger, pain, nausea) and immediate judgments, but not to other characters' thoughts. Free indirect discourse blends the narrator's voice with the character's internal register, including profanity and slang.

## Does Not
1. Use semicolons to connect clauses
2. Describe weather or setting without a character's direct perception
3. Employ flashbacks or extended backstory
4. Use adverbs to modify dialogue tags
5. Write paragraphs longer than eight sentences
6. Include omniscient narration or authorial commentary
7. Use rhetorical questions in narration
8. Describe characters' facial expressions in isolation from action
