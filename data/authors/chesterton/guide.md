# Working Guide to This Author's Prose Style

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences vary widely, from crisp declarative statements under ten words to elaborate constructions exceeding fifty words. Longer sentences typically accumulate through serial clauses joined by semicolons, dashes, or coordinating conjunctions, often building toward a pointed conclusion. Short sentences frequently deliver punchlines, revelations, or shifts in action.

## Syntax Habits
The author favors parallel structures and balanced constructions, often pairing contrasting elements within a single sentence. Subordinate clauses beginning with "though," "while," and "as" regularly introduce concessive or simultaneous actions. Sentences frequently begin with participial phrases or temporal markers ("As he spoke," "When they went"). Questions appear sparingly but carry weight when used.

## Diction and Register
Vocabulary is formal but not academic, with a preference for concrete nouns and active verbs. Abstract concepts are rendered through physical metaphors. Characters speak in distinct registers: aristocrats use polished, slightly archaic phrasing; working-class figures speak more directly; educated characters employ precise, sometimes pedantic vocabulary. The narrator maintains a consistent, slightly elevated tone regardless of subject.

## Punctuation
Semicolons are frequent, linking closely related clauses. Dashes appear for interruptions, afterthoughts, and dramatic pauses. Quotation marks enclose dialogue with attribution often inserted mid-sentence. Exclamation marks are used sparingly but for strong emotion. Commas regularly separate serial elements and introductory phrases.

## Figurative Habits
Similes and metaphors are concrete and often drawn from nature, mechanics, or domestic life: comparisons to animals, machines, household objects. Figurative language tends toward the grotesque or incongruous, pairing unlikely elements for effect. Extended metaphors are rare; most figures are single, striking images.

## Paragraphing
Paragraphs are typically short to medium length, rarely exceeding eight sentences. Action sequences use very short paragraphs, sometimes single sentences. Descriptive or expository passages build longer paragraphs. Dialogue is often embedded within narrative paragraphs rather than isolated, with attribution woven into action.

## Dialogue Handling
Attribution tags ("said X," "cried Y") are frequent and varied, placed mid-sentence or after the first phrase. Characters' speech reveals class, education, and temperament through vocabulary and syntax. Interruptions and overlapping speech are common. Dialogue often carries argumentative or expository weight, advancing plot or revealing character.

## Narration and Perspective
Third-person limited, shifting among characters but anchored most often to a central observer. The narrator occasionally offers wry commentary or ironic asides. Physical description precedes psychological revelation; interior states are inferred from external action. The narrative voice maintains a detached, slightly amused tone even in serious moments.

## Does Not
- Use first-person narration
- Employ stream of consciousness or interior monologue
- Write dialect phonetically
- Use chapter breaks or numbered sections
- Include explicit sexual content
- Describe characters' physical appearance in exhaustive detail
- Use present tense for narrative
- Employ footnotes or endnotes
- Break the fourth wall or address the reader directly
