# Working Guide to This Author's Prose

## Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences vary widely, from clipped fragments ("Cold! Cold!") to long, flowing constructions of thirty to fifty words. Short sentences often follow longer ones for emphasis or emotional punctuation. Many sentences begin with coordinating conjunctions ("And," "But," "So"), creating a cumulative, breathless quality.

## Syntax Habits
Frequent use of present participles to begin sentences or clauses ("Flinging her small scarf," "Threading her way," "Stirring the cup"). Heavy reliance on apposition and paired phrases separated by dashes or commas. Sentences often list actions in sequence without subordination, giving a paratactic, moment-by-moment feel. Questions appear frequently, both direct and rhetorical.

## Diction and Register
Colloquial, conversational diction with sudden shifts into lyrical or elevated language. Characters use contractions, interjections ("Oh," "Ah," "Well"), and informal tags ("you know," "of course"). Physical sensations dominate: cold, warmth, smells, textures. Abstract emotions are rendered through concrete, domestic imagery.

## Punctuation
Dashes are the primary structural punctuation—used for interruption, afterthought, apposition, and dramatic pause. Ellipses indicate trailing off or unspoken thought. Exclamation marks are frequent for emotional intensity. Semicolons are rare; commas and dashes do the work. Italics for emphasis, internal voices, or song lyrics.

## Figurative Habits
Similes drawn from domestic and natural worlds: "like little children," "like a needle," "like a ghost," "like bright birds." Personification of objects and weather: the sun "thieved its way in," leaves "wriggled like fishes." Extended metaphors (the sleigh ride, the garden party) are sustained through a passage. Figurative language is precise, not decorative.

## Paragraphing
Short paragraphs, often a single sentence or two. Dialogue is separated into its own paragraphs, with speech attributions embedded or following. Paragraph breaks mark shifts in focus, character, or time. Action and thought are often compressed into one paragraph.

## Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is rendered without quotation marks in some passages, with them in others. Speech tags are minimal ("said," "cried," "whispered") and often placed after the speech. Characters interrupt themselves and each other. Internal thought is blended with spoken dialogue without clear demarcation.

## Narration and Perspective
Close third-person limited, shifting between characters' consciousnesses. Free indirect discourse is the dominant mode—thoughts and perceptions blend into narration without "she thought" tags. The narrator adopts the character's vocabulary and emotional register. Shifts between characters occur at paragraph breaks.

## Does Not
1. Use long, periodic sentences with multiple subordinate clauses
2. Employ formal or Latinate diction
3. Write extended descriptive passages without human presence
4. Use omniscient narration that judges characters
5. Provide explicit psychological analysis or authorial commentary
6. Construct elaborate plot machinery or foreshadowing
7. Use dialogue tags more elaborate than "said" or "cried"
8. Write paragraphs longer than eight to ten lines
