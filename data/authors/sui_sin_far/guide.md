### Sentence Rhythm and Length
Sentences vary widely, from short declarative bursts to long, flowing constructions that often exceed thirty words. Longer sentences are typically built with coordinating conjunctions ("and," "but") and participial phrases, creating a cumulative, additive rhythm. Short sentences frequently follow emotional climaxes or shifts in action, providing emphasis.

### Syntax Habits
The author frequently uses inverted or fronted structures for emphasis: adverbial phrases ("For the first time since she had left her home"), participial openings ("Propping her chin on her hands"), and object-fronting ("Well could she remember"). Parallelism is common, especially in lists or paired clauses ("Why, oh why, had she broken her wing? Why, oh why, were brothers and sisters so?"). Exclamatory and interrogative sentences are used liberally, often to convey character emotion.

### Diction and Register
Diction is generally simple and concrete, with occasional formal or archaic flourishes ("beneficent spirit," "honorable great-grandfather"). The register shifts between colloquial dialogue (including dialectal contractions like "Me 'shamed") and a more elevated, almost fairy-tale-like narrative voice. Repetition of key words ("little," "honorable," "sad") creates a rhythmic, incantatory effect.

### Punctuation
Dashes and ellipses are used frequently to indicate interruption, hesitation, or trailing off in dialogue. Exclamation marks are abundant, especially in emotional passages. Commas are used to separate clauses in long sentences, but the author often prefers semicolons to link closely related independent clauses. Quotation marks enclose dialogue, with speech tags often placed mid-sentence.

### Figurative Habits
Similes and metaphors are drawn from nature and domestic life: eyes "as bright as jade jewels," a face with "little red rivers," a bird's wing "broken." Personification is common (the sea is "lovely and loving," the leaves "dance"). Figurative language is often simple and childlike, reinforcing themes of innocence and longing.

### Paragraphing
Paragraphs are generally short, often only two to five sentences. They frequently break at shifts in speaker, action, or emotional state. Dialogue is often separated into its own paragraphs, with each new speaker starting a new line. Narrative paragraphs are compact, focusing on a single image or moment.

### Dialogue Handling
Dialogue is rendered with minimal speech tags, often using "said he" or "replied she" in inverted order. Characters' speech is marked by distinctive idioms (e.g., "Me 'shamed," "Honorable great-grandfather"). Interruptions and overlapping speech are indicated by dashes. Dialogue often carries the emotional weight of the scene, with narrative description kept brief.

### Narration and Perspective
Third-person limited narration is used, shifting focalization between characters, often within the same story. The narrator occasionally addresses the reader directly ("You see," "Now, what the little Chinese seabird had seen"). The tone is sympathetic and slightly moralizing, with a clear but understated authorial stance. Perspective is often childlike or naive, even when the subject is adult.

### Does Not
1. Use long, complex subordinate clauses that delay the main verb.
2. Employ free indirect discourse or stream of consciousness.
3. Write dialogue without attribution for extended stretches.
4. Use abstract or Latinate vocabulary for emotional description.
5. Describe physical settings in extensive, detailed paragraphs.
6. Shift tense within a scene (consistently past tense).
7. Use irony or sarcasm in the narrative voice.
8. Include explicit political or historical commentary.
